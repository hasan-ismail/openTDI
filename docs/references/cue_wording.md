# Cue wording: research brief (2026-10-30)

Final wording: Cues.kt and GuideScript.kt, 2026-10-30.

Sources are academic only. Every number was read in the full text unless it is marked **[abstract only]** or **[second-hand]**. **[weak]** means a small study, no control group, or self-report only. "Inference" and "judgment" are mine, not a study's. Numbers in [brackets] point to the source list at the end.

## Bottom line

1. **In every study, "You are dreaming" was played only while the person was asleep in REM** [7][8][9]. Words spoken to someone who is awake or falling asleep were either a theme ("Remember to think of a tree"), a question ("what's going through your mind"), or practice for the future ("As you notice the cue, you become lucid"; "next time I'm dreaming, I will remember I'm dreaming") [1][3][4][10]. So OpenTDI's "You're falling asleep. You are dreaming now." contradicts itself, and it is also false when it plays: WAKE_PROMPT wakes the person, and INITIAL/RE_INCUBATION play while they are awake or drifting off. *(Inference from the study protocols.)*
2. **The cue that worked best in the lab did not say "you are dreaming". It told people to check:** "remain critically aware, lucid, and notice how aspects of this experience are in any way different from your normal waking experience" [4][5]. One participant noticed the cues in a dream in which she was lying awake in the lab, and so assumed she was awake. Another checked, saw that her hair was up when it had been down, and realised she was dreaming [4] **[anecdote]**.
3. **Safety:** no study has tested phrases like "nothing is real". The closest evidence argues against them. Deliberate induction predicted a rise in dissociation, including depersonalization/derealization, over about two months [13] **[weak]**. Reviewers warn that repeatedly questioning reality "may cause confusion" [14]. "Unreal" and "dreamy" surroundings are how derealization itself is defined [22]. Scope the claim to the dream ("this is a dream") and add a safety statement ("you are safe in bed"). Calling sleep paralysis "common, benign, and temporary" is the reappraisal step of the only tested sleep-paralysis therapy [20][21] **[weak, n=10]**.

## Q2. Safety of "nothing is real" style messages

- **No study has tested telling sleepers "nothing is real" or "this is not reality"** at sleep onset or at any other time. **[gap]**
- **Induction and dissociation:** 78 undergraduates were followed for about 2 months. More deliberate induction predicted rises in DES-II dissociation (β=.26, p<.01), including the depersonalization/derealization subscale (β=.25, p<.05), and in schizotypy (β=.14, p<.05). Spontaneous lucid dreaming did not [13] **[weak; self-report, students; the 2025 review says "3-month"]**.
- **Mechanism the reviewers propose:** reality testing "promotes excessive preoccupation with one's state, which may cause confusion", like obsessive checking. "A person who is unsure of what is real versus unreal to begin with, may not necessarily benefit from being taught to question this several times a day." Uncertainty "may stem primarily from the intensity of using the technique." The reviewers suggest "detecting cues to solidify that this is a dream" in place of methods that "may confuse sleep-wake boundaries (e.g., wake-back-to-bed, constant reality testing)" [14] **[review; opinion]**.
- **Earlier caution:** induction methods involve "deliberate confusion of the sleep and waking states", e.g. asking "am I dreaming?" by day [12] **[opinion]**.
- **Lucid dreamers and blurred boundaries:** of 489 lucid dreamers, about 4% named blurred dream–wake boundaries as a downside. Lucid-dream frequency went with higher derealization but not with overall dissociation [15] **[abstract + via the review in [14]; cross-sectional]**. People high in dream awareness showed a more liberal reality-monitoring bias (18 vs 13 people) [17] **[weak]**.
- **Forum analysis (n=174 posts):** 15% described "reality confusion", mostly false awakenings, "largely benign". Sleep paralysis appeared in 28% and was often tied to MILD attempts. Some users said that knowing about sleep paralysis made it less frightening. Distressing lucid dreams went with **low dream control** [16] **[weak; self-selected forum posts]**.
- **Broken sleep alone raises dissociation:** one night without sleep increased dissociative symptoms (n=25) [18]. The authors' model is a "labile sleep-wake cycle" in which "dreamlike mentation invades the waking state" [19] **[review]**. That this applies to Dormio's repeated wakings is my inference.
- **Definition:** derealization is "the perception of other people, objects, or the world as strange or unreal", "dreamy" [22]. "Nothing is real" said to someone who is awake describes that symptom. *(Inference.)*
- **Who should not use it:** the Dormio Light authors advise screening out psychosis, narcolepsy, isolated hypnagogic or hypnopompic hallucinations, recurrent sleep paralysis and parasomnias [3]. That study reported no adverse events.
- **Sleep paralysis framing:** MR therapy tells the person "the experience is common, benign, and temporary", that the hallucinations are "a typical byproduct of REM mentation", and that there is "no reason to be afraid" [20]. In a pilot, days with sleep paralysis fell by 50% (n=6, vs 4 controls) [21] **[weak]**. This supports a "you are safe" line. No study has tested "you are safe" inside a cue. **[gap]**
- **Nightmares:** in the TLR study, people with higher nightmare distress incorporated more cues and had more negative emotion in their lucid dreams (R=.41). The cued group had less negative emotion than the uncued group [4] **[weak; n=28]**.

## Q3. Wording that helps people recognise a dream

- **Prompt a check, don't only state a fact.** Carr's authors credit the "notice whether aspects of this experience differ from normal waking experience" step. 92% of people with verified lucid dreams noticed a cue in the dream, against 46% of the others, so noticing the cue is "important but not sufficient" [4] **[moderate; n=28 cued]**.
- **Statements alone gave small effects:** "You are dreaming" in REM gave 6% vs 3% for the name [7], and 3 of 11 [8] **[weak]**. A 2026 study of auditory-triggered reality checks found 1 lucid dream in 15 people (*IJoDR*, "Revisiting auditory-triggered reality checks in lucid dream induction") **[not opened; title and result from search only]**.
- **MILD has people picture "noticing something unusual"** and rehearse "recognizing that this anomaly only occurs in dreams" [10][11]. Daytime reality testing added nothing in ILDIS [10].
- **No study compares cue wordings head to head** ("You're dreaming" vs "Is this a dream?" vs "Notice something strange"). **[gap]**

## Implications for the texts (inference and judgment, not tested)

- **Rule:** tell people they are dreaming only when the cue will be heard in a dream. Make the claim conditional ("if you hear this in a dream…") or about the future (MILD-style). Never say it to someone who is being woken.
- **Scope it:** say "this is a dream" and "the dream can't hurt you", not "nothing is real". Pair it with "you are safe in bed".
- **Keep it short:** one to three short sentences, each a full sentence, with no jargon ("cue") and no stacked commas, so text-to-speech reads it well.
- **Draft lines:**
  - INITIAL_INCUBATION: "Soon you'll be dreaming. If you hear my voice in a dream, it is a dream. You are safe in bed."
  - WAKE_PROMPT: "You were drifting off. What did you notice?" The owner's saved MIT line, "You're falling asleep. Please tell me, what's going through your mind?", matches [1] §2.4 word for word and is truthful, so it can stay.
  - RE_INCUBATION: "You can fall back asleep now. Next time you're dreaming, you'll notice it's a dream."
  - SESSION_END: "That was the last one for tonight. Sleep well."
  - Guide CUE_INTRO: "When you hear this sound tonight, notice what's around you. If anything is strange, you're dreaming." The current wording says "when you hear this sound… you are dreaming", but the sound also plays while the person is awake during training.
  - Guide PROMPT_AWARE: "Stay aware. Notice how this feels different from being awake." (plain-English version of [4][5])
  - Guide SSILD opener: "Now move your attention between three things: what you see, what you hear, and what you feel."
  - Keep the MILD line in the first person and word for word: "Next time I'm dreaming, I will remember I'm dreaming." [10]
